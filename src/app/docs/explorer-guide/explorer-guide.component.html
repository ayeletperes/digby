Guide

Repertoire & Genome Explorer

The Explorer puts the two VDJbase databases, genomic and AIRR-seq, behind one selection and eight analyses of it. This page describes what each analysis computes, how to read it, and what it deliberately does not claim. It is a companion to the short note behind the ⓘ control beside every panel title, not a replacement for it.

The Explorer is at /dash_refbook.

How the page is arranged

A rail on the left and one large canvas on the right. The rail lists the analyses first and the filters below them; the canvas holds whichever analysis is open. Arriving without naming a panel shows a gallery of cards instead, one per analysis, and Home at the top of the rail goes back to it.

The whole selection lives in the query string. Species, locus, segment, databases, projects, samples, genes, the drilled allele and the open panel are all in the URL, so a view can be linked to and the browser's back button steps back through an exploration rather than leaving the page.

The rail: one selection, shared by every analysis

The filters are not properties of the analysis you happen to have open. There is one selection, and every panel is drawn from it, which is what makes moving between panels a change of view rather than a change of question. A control that does not reach the panel currently open is dimmed and carries the reason, rather than being disabled: choosing a gene while looking at the locus map is a reasonable thing to do, and the other panels will use it.

Dataset

Species and locus, then two more controls.

Segment
V, D, J or C, with the number of genes in each. The segment is read from the fourth character of the gene name (IGHV1-2 gives V); there is no separate field for it in the API. Choosing a segment narrows the gene list and starts the new segment with one gene selected, the way a new locus starts.
Data
Which of the two databases to read, genomic or AIRR-seq or both. A database this locus does not hold is disabled with that reason, and at least one must stay on. Turning one off changes which projects and samples exist, so those lists are refetched.

Samples

Project and Sample, both multi-select, and both grouped under the database that holds them. This grouping matters: the two databases name entirely different studies, so a selection made in one narrows only that one. A database with none of the selection is left unfiltered rather than reporting zero, and the panel says which databases were actually narrowed in the note above its figure.

Seen in

Two sliders, one per database, setting a minimum number of samples an allele must be seen in. A slider appears only for a database being read, and only when the selected genes reach more than one sample in it.

  • The range is taken from the data, not fixed, so it spans exactly what the selected genes actually reach. Changing gene rebuilds it and the thresholds are clamped to the new maximum rather than silently excluding everything.
  • The handle moves between stops rather than over the raw range. Counts are heavily skewed, most alleles sitting in one or two samples while a few reach several hundred, so every value up to 12 gets its own detent and the long tail is compressed onto a ladder.
  • The threshold resolves to a list of allele names and travels as the alleles parameter, so every panel honours it, including the alignment, which is rendered on the server.
  • If nothing clears the thresholds the canvas says so and offers a reset. An empty allele list on the wire would read as "no filter" and show everything, which would contradict the rail.

Genes

Search and family grouping, because a flat list does not work when rhesus IGH holds hundreds of genes in one locus. At most three genes can be selected: each one is drawn as its own facet, its own component instance and its own request, and beyond a few the faceted view stops being readable. Each facet heading carries a focus control that opens that gene on its own.

Chips

Every active filter appears as a removable chip above the figure. Species and locus are not chips: they are the dataset being looked at rather than something narrowing it, and removing them would leave nothing to show.

The eight analyses

Grouped in the rail as Locus, Reference and Detail. A panel that needs a database the locus does not hold, or that you have switched off, is listed with the reason rather than hidden.

Locus map

Whole locus. Ignores the gene, sample and Data filters.

What it shows. Every allele in the locus as nested rings: chain, gene type, subgroup, gene, allele, from the centre out. An arc is as wide as the number of alleles beneath it. Four rings are drawn at once and drilling into a gene reveals its alleles, because at full locus width an allele arc is about a pixel across and no label fits in one.

How to read it. Click a ring to drill in, click the same ring again to step back out, click an allele to open it in the Allele panel. The breadcrumb above the figure shows where you are and Reset returns to the whole locus. Drilled to a gene, the map offers Explore <gene> →, which selects that gene and opens the Overview. Colour marks the drill position, not anything about the data. Hovering an allele says which databases hold it and whether it is in the baseline reference set; hovering a branch counts the alleles beneath it, how many are not in the baseline set, and how many are held in each database.

What it does not claim. Every number on this figure counts alleles, never samples or subjects. Samples cannot be added across alleles without counting the same subject many times, whereas an allele belongs to exactly one node at every level. The ring is also not a physical map: arcs are ordered V, D, J, C and then by gene name read numerically, not by position in the locus.

This is the reference content of the locus, so it is drawn from both databases whatever the Data toggle says, and no gene, sample or "seen in" filter narrows it. Pseudogenes and orphons (names containing /OR) are excluded, and on the genomic side only sequences recorded as Functional or ORF are taken.

Overview

One gene. Uses every filter.

What it shows. Every allele recorded for the gene and how many samples each was seen in, as one horizontal bar per database side by side. An allele held in both databases has two bars, and their heights can differ because the databases hold different samples. A database you have not selected is omitted rather than drawn empty, and a genuine zero draws no bar at all rather than a floor-height one.

Above the chart: the number of alleles, how many are in the baseline reference set, how many are novel, and a box giving the size of each cohort. That box states whether any sample was sequenced both ways, and opens a per-project breakdown of where the samples come from.

How to read it. Rows sort by most carried or by name. Clicking a bar opens that allele. The counts are of samples carrying the allele, counted from the sample tables at request time so that they agree with the filters and with the carrier figures elsewhere; they are not the whole-dataset totals written when the database was built.

The Both bar. Where it appears, it is a true intersection: samples that carry the allele in the genomic and the AIRR-seq database. That is only possible where the same subject is in both cohorts. In human the two cohorts are disjoint, so Both is necessarily zero and the series is not drawn at all; the cohort box says "Separate cohorts, no overlap". In rhesus macaque the same animals were sequenced both ways, so Both is meaningful there.

Novel and baseline. "Novel" means not in the baseline reference set, and nothing more; baseline is simply the remainder. Deletion markers, the *Del names, are counted as baseline alleles even though no sample carries a sequence for them. They are genotype states rather than alleles, and they dominate the rows that no sample carries. The toolbar says how many such rows there are and points at the "seen in" sliders as the way to drop them.

Allele names

One gene.

What it shows. Every allele of the gene with its full name, the label the plots draw it under, and the number of germline substitutions its name records. This is the key to the shortened labels on every other figure. Clicking a name opens that allele.

How to read it. See Allele labels below for the rule. A dash in the "Shown as" column means the name is short enough to be drawn in full.

What it does not claim. The Differences column counts the substitutions written into the allele's name. Those are germline differences from a reference allele. They are not somatic hypermutation, and nothing in the Explorer measures somatic hypermutation.

Alignment

One gene.

What it shows. Every allele of the gene aligned against the first one alphabetically, which acts as the reference row. Dashes mean the codon matches the reference, lower-case letters mark a substitution, and dots are IMGT alignment gaps. Numbers above the rows are codon positions and, for V genes, the CDR regions are marked.

How to read it. Codons per line is adjustable, and the text can be copied or downloaded as a plain text file. Names longer than 26 characters are shortened, with a legend above the alignment giving the originals: the format indents every sequence past the longest name, and a single long name would push the sequences hundreds of columns to the right.

What it does not claim. Drilling into a single allele keeps the gene's first allele as the reference row, since a lone sequence has nothing to be compared against. Rows with no stored sequence, and the *Del deletion markers, are not in the alignment at all.

The alignment is rendered on the server by receptor_utils.sequence_alignment rather than reimplemented in the browser, so it cannot drift from the reference implementation. It is memoised per database build.

Group clustering

One gene.

What it shows. The alleles of the gene grouped by how much their sequences differ, drawn as a dendrogram with the distance on the x axis, labelled "Distance (nt)".

This is not a phylogenetic tree. A tree claims descent. This groups alleles by sequence similarity and says nothing about ancestry: two alleles one position apart group closely whether or not one came from the other.

Complete linkage. Two groups join at the distance of their furthest pair. So where a join sits on the axis is that distance, and it is not each allele's distance to the rest of its group.

Two different distances, and the note under the figure says which was used.

  • V. V alleles are stored IMGT-gapped, so the columns correspond by construction and the distance is a count of the columns at which two alleles differ. A terminal gap is unrecorded sequence, not a difference: an allele recorded only from position 20 onwards must not read as 19 substitutions against a full-length one. Internal gaps are kept, because those are real indels. A novel allele carrying an insertion or deletion shifts every later column, so those rows are put back into the gene's own column frame and the inserted columns, which the frame cannot represent, are added back by their length. The note also says how many of the gapped positions actually vary between these alleles.
  • D and J. These have no gapped form and differ in length, so each pair is aligned end to end with a Needleman-Wunsch global alignment and the distance is an edit distance: an inserted or deleted base counts the same as a substituted one. End gaps are penalised here, unlike the V path, because nothing distinguishes an unrecorded end from a genuinely shorter allele, and free end gaps would score every pair as identical.

How to read it. Clicking a leaf marker opens that allele; clicking a branch does nothing. Alleles that are identical over the compared positions stay as separate leaves joined at distance zero and are also listed above the figure, so no allele silently disappears. With only one allele in the selection there is nothing to cluster and the panel says so.

The downloads are the linkage itself, not the drawing: a table in scipy's Z format (which two clusters joined, at what distance, and how many alleles that left) and a short script that redraws it with scipy.cluster.hierarchy.dendrogram.

Allele Usage

One gene. Needs AIRR-seq data.

What it shows. The relative usage of each allele: one horizontal box per allele, one point per sample. A sample in which the allele was not detected contributes no point, so a box describes the samples where the allele was seen and not the whole cohort.

The denominator is the locus, not the gene. A point is the allele's assigned count divided by the total assigned count of every allele of every gene in that sample, so it is the share of the sample's repertoire that this one allele accounts for. That is why the alleles of a single gene do not sum to 1: across 361 samples, the alleles of IGHV1-18 together account for a median of 1.2% of the repertoire, and IGHV3-33 for 0.8%. Two alleles are therefore comparable across genes as well as within one.

How to read it. Individual sample points can be turned off to read the boxes alone. Clicking a point adds that sample to the filter; clicking the box itself opens the allele. Where the allele names are long, the axis carries shortened labels and a legend above the figure gives the originals.

What it does not claim. The panel says what is plotted and stops there. What a narrow box, or a wide one, means about the allele is a reading the panel does not make for you.

Zygosity

One gene. Either database.

What it shows. Which combinations of alleles occur together in the same subject, as an UpSet plot. Each column is a combination, its bar counts the samples carrying exactly that set, and the dots below say which alleles are in it. The bars on the left are how often each allele appears overall, regardless of what it appears with.

The two databases are unioned per subject. The unit of zygosity is the subject, not the record, so the sets are keyed on sample name across both databases. Where the same subject was sequenced both ways, as the rhesus macaque cohort was, the two databases describe one animal and must not become two rows. Where the cohorts are disjoint, as in the human data, the union is simply a concatenation.

How to read it. Zygosity is which alleles a subject carries, which the genomic database reports as well as the AIRR-seq one, so this panel is not gated on AIRR-seq: only usage is. Drilling into an allele keeps the carriers of that allele and shows their whole sets, which is what makes the plot answer what an allele occurs alongside.

The chart draws shortened labels, but the downloads under it carry the alleles under their full names, since a downloaded file is something someone will join on.

Allele

One allele. Opened by clicking an allele in any plot.

What it shows. The level below the gene, assembled from the same endpoints the other panels use, each narrowed to one allele:

  • Where it was seen: how many genomic and how many AIRR-seq samples, and whether the project selection actually narrowed each database.
  • Usage: its median share of a repertoire and the number of samples behind that, plotted per project so a project that behaves differently is visible.
  • Carriers: how many subjects carry it, how many carry it as their only allele of the gene, and the carriers per project split by database.
  • Occurs alongside: the other alleles of the gene found in the same subjects, and an UpSet plot of the combinations among its carriers. The allele itself is dropped from those sets: every carrier has it by definition, so keeping it would add one set covering the whole chart.
  • Sequence: the allele against the gene's reference allele.

What it does not claim. Opening an allele does not narrow the gene-level panels to it. An Overview of a gene filtered to one allele is a chart with one bar, which is not an overview of anything, so the gene-level panels stay as they were and stepping back up is one click.

Drilling between panels

Clicks inside a figure are how you move between levels. None of them is destructive: every one lands in the URL and can be undone with its chip or with the browser's back button, and clicking the same thing again steps back out.

  • An allele, in any plot, opens the Allele panel. Clicking the same allele again steps back up to the Overview.
  • A sample, from a point in the usage boxplot, is added to the sample filter.
  • A gene, from the locus map, means "take me to this gene": it is selected and the Overview opens. From a gene-level panel a gene only narrows the selection, without changing panel.

Only the gene ring of the locus map leads anywhere. A subgroup is not something the other panels take, and an allele already opens its own panel.

Allele labels

VDJbase writes every germline substitution of a novel allele into its name, which takes the longest names past 190 characters against a median of about 23. No chart axis can carry that, so long names are shortened, by one rule shared by the whole application.

IGHV1-18*04_t81a_t90c_a106g_g112t → IGHV1-18*04+4~i93

  • A name of 26 characters or fewer is drawn in full.
  • Otherwise the name is cut at the first underscore after the allele, not at the first underscore in the string: gene names themselves contain underscores, as in IGHV4-NL_1*01_a157g, and cutting at the first one drops the allele and miscounts the substitutions.
  • What follows is +N, the number of substitutions in the suffix, and ~ plus a three-character token. The token is an FNV-1a hash of the allele's own suffix in base 36. The count alone is not distinguishing: a large minority of the names long enough to be shortened share a stem and a count with another allele. Deriving the token from the allele's own suffix also means it never moves when more data is loaded, which a positional numbering would.
  • A long name with no suffix at all is truncated and marked with a trailing ~.
  • Any remaining clash is disambiguated with #2, #3 and so on. Both sides sort the names before assigning, so a #2 lands on the same allele in the browser as it does on the server, which receive their names in different orders.

The rule is mirrored byte for byte between shared/models/gene-naming.ts in the client and abbreviate_names in api/refbook/refbook.py on the server. It has to be: the alignment's labels are rendered into server-side text and a divergence could not be fixed in the browser.

The label is a label. The stored name is the identity that every join uses and is never rewritten. When you need the real name, the Allele names panel is the key, and the clustering, usage and alignment panels each carry a legend for the names they shortened.

The two databases

Almost everything surprising in the Explorer comes from one fact: the genomic and AIRR-seq databases are separate builds over different sample sets, and how different depends on the species.

  • Human. The two cohorts are disjoint. They share no sample and no project. A "Both" count is therefore necessarily zero, and the genomic and AIRR-seq sample counts add up.
  • Rhesus macaque. One cohort sequenced both ways, with the sample names matching across the two databases. An allele really can be seen in the same animal by both, so "Both" is meaningful, and counting the animals twice would be wrong. The zygosity union and the site's headline sample count both key on sample name for this reason.

The same fact drives the project and sample lists, which are grouped under the database that holds each entry, and the scope note above each figure, which says which databases the selection actually narrowed.

The macaque figures quoted in the code are 106 of 106 sample names matching. Whether that holds for every species and locus in a given deployment is a property of the data loaded, not of the code, and the Overview's cohort box reports it for whatever is loaded.

Taking a figure away

Every figure offers two downloads beside the standard "Download plot as a PNG" in the Plotly toolbar: Data (TSV) and Python. Where a figure has no Plotly toolbar, which is the case for the zygosity UpSet plot and the charts inside the Allele panel, the same two sit as buttons under the figure.

Data (TSV)
One row per drawn point, tab separated. The rows are read off the traces the panel handed to the chart, so what was filtered out was never in them: the download is the figure on screen, filters and ordering included, and there is no second data path that can disagree with it. Column headings come from the axis titles.
Python
A runnable script that redraws the same figure with pandas and matplotlib. The rows are embedded rather than fetched, so it is the figure as drawn and it runs on a machine that cannot reach the server; the endpoint is named in a comment for re-querying.

Two panels override both, because their traces are not their data. The clustering panel's traces are line vertices, so it exports the linkage matrix and a scipy dendrogram script instead of the drawing. The zygosity panel exports one row per combination, with the alleles under their full names, and a script using upsetplot.

The alignment is text rather than a figure, and has its own Copy and Download controls in its toolbar.

What the Explorer does not claim

Group clustering is not phylogeny. It groups by sequence similarity under complete linkage. No node is an ancestor and no height is a divergence time.

The locus map is not a genomic map. Arcs are ordered by segment and then by gene name; arc width counts alleles, not physical extent and not samples.

Suffixes in allele names are germline variation. A name like IGHV1-18*04_a157g records where that germline allele differs from its reference allele. Nothing here measures somatic hypermutation.

Novel means "not in the baseline reference set". It is a statement about the reference set, not about how recently or how rarely the allele arose. An allele called novel in either database is marked novel, because the two databases are built separately and only one of them may have seen it.

Absence in a panel is not absence from the data. Two filters remove alleles quietly by design: the "seen in" thresholds, and restricting the Data toggle to one database. The scope note above each figure and the chips above the canvas are what say so.

Not everything is offered. Pseudogenes and orphons are excluded from the gene lists and the locus map; on the genomic side only sequences recorded as Functional or ORF are read; and IGHC is not offered as a locus.

Where this is implemented

For anyone checking a claim on this page against the code:

  • Panel registry, and the caption behind each ⓘ: src/app/dash-refbook/dash-panels.ts.
  • The rail, the filters, the URL state and the drill routing: src/app/dash-refbook/dash-refbook.component.ts.

  • The two downloads: src/app/shared/plot-export/plot-export.ts.
